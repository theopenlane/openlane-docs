# Standards

Standards represent compliance frameworks and regulations that organizations must adhere to. Openlane supports a wide range of industry standards including SOC 2, ISO 27001, NIST frameworks, and industry-specific regulations.

## What is a Standard?

A Standard in Openlane represents a formal compliance framework, regulation, or set of guidelines that defines security, privacy, or operational requirements. Standards provide the foundation for compliance programs by defining the controls and requirements that organizations must implement.

Standards are established by industry associations, government bodies, or regulatory agencies. Some carry legal force, such as GDPR and HIPAA, while others are voluntary frameworks that customers and auditors expect, such as SOC 2 and ISO 27001.

## Compliance Significance

In a compliance program, the standard is where requirements come from: it defines the controls you implement, the criteria auditors assess against, and the certification or attestation you can earn (an ISO 27001 certificate, a SOC 2 report) to demonstrate compliance to customers.

## Types of Compliance Standards

Compliance standards typically address information security, privacy, risk management, and governance aspects of an organization. Here is a breakdown of the types of compliance standards:

| Type | Description | Examples |
| ---- | ----------- | -------- |
| Regulatory compliance | Mandated by law; compliance with these frameworks is not optional | GDPR, HIPAA, PCI DSS |
| Industry-specific compliance | Developed by industry associations as a set of best practices for a particular industry | NIST cybersecurity for the technology sector, FISMA for federal agencies |
| Operational compliance | Focused on ensuring reliability, integrity, and efficiency of an organization's operations and processes | SOC 2, ISO 27001, COBIT for IT and governance |

## Working with Standards in Openlane

Every framework above follows the same pattern in Openlane. Clone a standard's controls from the catalog into your organization, or create a [program](/docs/platform/compliance-management/programs/overview) that adds them automatically and bundles them for an audit period. When you add a second framework, [import and map your existing controls](/docs/platform/compliance-management/controls/import) to the new requirements instead of building a parallel set: one well-implemented control can satisfy several frameworks at once, and the mappings are what make that count toward each of them.

Most framework pages also include an Example Evidence page listing the artifacts that typically satisfy manually evidenced control areas.

## How do you choose the right compliance framework for your business?

We recommend weighing the following factors when deciding which frameworks to pursue:

| Factor | Consideration |
| ------ | ------------- |
| Industry | The frameworks your business should follow will depend on your industry. For example, HIPAA is mandatory for healthcare while PCI DSS is for those who handle credit card data. |
| Geography | If you process personal data from the EU, or have personal data collected from California, you will need GDPR and CCPA compliance, respectively. |
| Customer requirements | Customers are data sensitive and may require compliance with specific standards as a prerequisite for doing business. For example, enterprises require their vendors to be SOC 2 compliant. |
| Legal requirements | The nature and jurisdiction of your business is another factor. Healthcare industries are required to be HIPAA compliant; companies handling data of EU citizens are required to be GDPR compliant. |
| Competitive advantage | International and widely recognized standards can differentiate your business and drive enterprise-level deals, setting you apart from competitors and showing invested parties your commitment to security and privacy. |

You can speak to key stakeholders like IT, legal, HR, and business heads to understand your business obligations and requirements. Once you have identified the relevant compliance standards and regulatory requirements for your business, develop a roadmap for implementation.

Keep in mind that compliance is not a one-time effort. Even with a single regulation to maintain, the work is continuous, and a simple oversight can result in severe consequences.

## Beyond fines, what are the consequences of noncompliance?

While hefty fines and penalties get most of the attention, noncompliance carries a cascade of other consequences for your organization.

In severe cases noncompliance can lead to lawsuits, and even if you prevail, they are very expensive to defend. Chegg, an edutech company, was sued by the FTC for over $40 million for lax data security practices after more than 4 security breaches since 2017 exposed private information the company held. Based on the severity of the noncompliance, regulatory bodies can also disbar your organization from specific activities.

Noncompliance limits growth. Many businesses require frameworks like SOC 2 or ISO 27001 as a prerequisite for doing business with them, and data breaches and cyberthreats, which usually stem from noncompliance, erode the trust your existing customers have placed in you while hindering your ability to acquire new ones. It also lengthens your sales cycle as you scramble to become compliant and demonstrate it to potential clients, giving your competition an edge and putting valuable deals at risk.
