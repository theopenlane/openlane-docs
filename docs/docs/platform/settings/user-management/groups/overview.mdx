# Groups

Groups do two jobs in Openlane: they organize your people, and they carry permissions. Put users in a group, give the group access to the right objects (programs, controls, policies, <Term t="evidence">evidence</Term>), and everyone in it can do their work without per-person permission bookkeeping.

Access control is itself a compliance requirement: auditors will ask who can edit controls, who can approve policies, and how you keep those lists current. Managing access through groups gives you an answer that is both operable and auditable: permissions live in one reviewable place, and membership changes leave a trail.

Groups also enforce segregation of duties: the people who write policies, the people who approve them, and the auditors who read everything should not hold the same permissions.

## Built-in groups

Every organization starts with three groups we maintain for you (**Viewers**, **Admins**, and **All Members**), kept in sync with each user's organization role automatically. Their membership is derived from each user's organization role rather than edited directly, so grants made through them never go stale: granting "All Members" view access to your policies takes one step and stays current as people join or leave.

See [System Managed Groups](./system-managed.mdx) for details.

## Organizing your own groups

There is no prescribed structure; create groups that match how your team actually divides the work. Common shapes:

| Shape | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Functional | A Security Team group that can edit controls and evidence, or a Compliance group that can approve policies |
| Project-based | A System and Organization Controls 2 (SOC 2) Audit group for this year's audit, with access scoped to that program and archived when the audit is done |
| Approval | A Risk Committee group whose members sign off on risk acceptance and treatment decisions |

We recommend starting small: the system-managed groups plus one or two functional groups cover most teams until an audit or a new framework gives you a reason for more structure.

## How permissions behave

Two rules explain most of what you will see. First, permissions inherit: access granted through a group flows to related objects, so an editor of a group can inherit editor access to programs associated with that group, and you do not re-grant permissions object by object.

Second, the most permissive grant wins. If someone is in one group that can edit a program and another that can only view it, they can edit it. When someone appears to have more access than a group suggests, check their other memberships.

The [Permissions](./permissions.mdx) page covers inheritance, overlap, and how to inspect effective access in depth.

## FAQ

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between an organization role and a group?</summary>

Your organization role (Member, Admin, Owner) sets your baseline access to the organization itself. Groups grant additional, object-level access on top: which controls you can edit, which programs you can see.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Someone has access I did not grant. Why?</summary>

This is almost always permission overlap: they are in another group with a more permissive grant, or they inherited access through a related object. Check their group memberships first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should auditors get their own group?</summary>

Yes. A read-only group scoped to the program under audit is the cleanest pattern. They see everything they need and cannot change any of it, and removing access after the audit is one membership change.

</details>
