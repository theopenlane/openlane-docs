# GRC Fundamentals

Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) is the coordinated strategy an organization uses to align its security policies, manage operational risk, and meet regulatory requirements. Understanding the concepts (what each discipline answers, how laws, regulations, and frameworks differ, and how audits run) makes the day-to-day objects in [Compliance Management](../compliance-management/overview.mdx) easier to use well.

## What is GRC?

The value of GRC is in the coordination: a GRC program gives business leaders, security teams, compliance officers, and risk managers one shared picture and clear accountability, instead of three departments solving overlapping problems independently.

## Why GRC Matters

- **Better decisions.** A structured program gives leadership real-time visibility into risks, compliance status, and internal controls, so decisions are made on data rather than in reaction to incidents. A gap analysis before an ISO 27001 audit, for example, tells you exactly which corrective actions to prioritize before the formal assessment.
- **Less wasted effort.** Manual compliance tracking in spreadsheets and email produces duplicate work, expensive audits, and time lost locating evidence when the auditor asks for it. Centralizing the program and automating the recurring pieces (evidence collection, access reviews, attestation campaigns) removes most of that overhead.
- **Reduced exposure.** Unmanaged third-party risk, weak access controls, and missed regulatory changes carry real costs in fines, breaches, and stalled deals. A proactive program surfaces these before they escalate.

There is also a commercial driver: enterprise buyers routinely require System and Organization Controls 2 (SOC 2), ISO 27001, or <Term t="general-data-protection-regulation">GDPR</Term> compliance before signing, and investors evaluate risk management as part of diligence. A program that can produce current attestations on demand (see [Trust Center](../trust-center/overview.mdx)) shortens those conversations.

## Who Owns GRC?

GRC is shared across security, IT, legal, and leadership, but who leads it day to day varies with organization size:

| Typical owner | Common in | Owns |
| ------------- | --------- | ---- |
| Chief information security officer (CISO) | Larger organizations | Strategic direction, executive alignment, oversight of risk and compliance initiatives |
| Head of GRC or Compliance | Mid-market and enterprise | Control implementation, audit readiness, internal policies |
| Engineering or DevSecOps lead | Smaller and tech-driven companies | Secure-by-design practices, automation, evidence collection |

Whoever leads, the program only works with executive buy-in: leadership sets the resourcing and treats compliance as a company-wide responsibility rather than an IT problem.

## Laws, Regulations, and Frameworks

Not everything called a "compliance requirement" carries the same force. The summary table on each page in [Frameworks & Standards](../standards/overview.mdx) classifies it as one of three types:

<div className="col col--4">
<div className="card">
<div className="card__header"><h4>Law</h4></div>
<div className="card__body">

**Created by:** legislatures

**Binding on:** everyone in the jurisdiction

**Enforced through:** courts and government action

**Consequences:** civil and criminal penalties, sometimes a private right to sue

**Style:** broad principles that agencies interpret

**Examples:** HIPAA, SOX, GDPR

</div>
</div>
</div>
<div className="col col--4">
<div className="card">
<div className="card__header"><h4>Regulation</h4></div>
<div className="card__body">

**Created by:** government agencies, under authority delegated by law

**Binding on:** entities the agency oversees

**Enforced through:** the agency itself

**Consequences:** fines, license loss, contract debarment

**Style:** more prescriptive than the underlying law

**Examples:** CMMC, DFARS, NY DFS Part 500

</div>
</div>
</div>
<div className="col col--4">
<div className="card">
<div className="card__header"><h4>Framework</h4></div>
<div className="card__body">

**Created by:** standards bodies and industry groups

**Binding on:** no one, until a customer or contract requires it

**Enforced through:** contracts and market expectations

**Consequences:** contractual and reputational

**Style:** control catalogs and best practices

**Examples:** ISO 27001, NIST CSF, SOC 2, CIS Controls, PCI DSS (via card network contracts)

</div>
</div>
</div>
</div>

The practical difference is who can compel you and what happens if you fall short: laws and regulations apply to you whether or not you agree, while frameworks become binding through the deals you sign. Most <Term t="compliance-program">compliance programs</Term> juggle all three at once, which is why one control mapped across several sources is the working unit of the [controls model](/docs/platform/compliance-management/controls/overview).

## GRC Frameworks

Beyond the security and privacy standards covered in [Frameworks & Standards](../standards/overview.mdx), a few published frameworks structure the GRC program itself. <Term t="committee-of-sponsoring-organizations-of-the-treadway-commission">COSO</Term>'s internal control framework underpins financial-reporting governance (it is what Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) compliance builds on, and the SOC 2 <Term t="trust-services-criteria">Trust Services Criteria</Term> are built on it as well). Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies (COBIT), from the Information Systems Audit and Control Association (ISACA), structures IT governance and separates governance from management. On the risk side, [Risk Management Frameworks](../exposure/risks/riskmanagement.mdx) surveys COSO Enterprise Risk Management (ERM), ISO 31000, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Risk Management Framework (RMF), Factor Analysis of Information Risk (FAIR), and others.

You do not need to adopt one of these wholesale; most organizations borrow the structure they need. What matters is that governance, risk, and compliance run on shared definitions and shared data, which is the practice the rest of this section and the [Glossary](../glossary/index.mdx) support.
